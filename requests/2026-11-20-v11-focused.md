## What this is

Nicolas's queue has a new version, `ubq::v11` in the crate: version 11 of UBQ, meant to take the place of the earlier ones if it proves better, and what the paper will be about. He needs five things from Grace and MareNostrum 5, soon: whether v11 delivers every element exactly once there, where it stands beside the other strict FIFO queues, what moves v11 itself (the batch, the block length, and what its counters say where it is slow), what each queue holds in memory, and what share of a steady stream of arriving elements each queue delivers. It has never run on more than 160 cores, on 64 KiB pages, or on a two-socket x86 machine.

**It is two jobs per cluster.** Each has one node for at most two hours and took 75 to 91 minutes at home: about 3 node-hours per cluster, at most 4 at the jobs' limits.

**It ends the request of 2026-10-30 and replaces the request of 2026-11-10.** The runs of 2026-10-30 that are still going on Grace (`ubq-dynblock`, `ubq-bitmask`, `ubq-v8`) measure queues Nicolas no longer needs, and most of their tasks time out. Step 0 keeps what they finished and cancels the rest. Do step 0 first: those jobs hold the account's priority and its submit quota.

It is built so that the step overhead of the earlier runs cannot stop it:
- **One job runs every plan** on one node, where the earlier lab ran one task per job and wrote five files per step to GPFS.
- **A job writes nothing to GPFS until it ends.** Everything it produces stays on its node's own disk (`$TMPDIR`): the binary, each trial's files, the result files and its logs. GPFS is read once, when a job starts, and written once, when it ends: the result files and the job's logs, copied and renamed into place. The one exception is the binary a job builds, which goes to GPFS as soon as it is built, so that the cluster's other job can take it. While a job runs, `$ROOT/results` and the job's directory under `$ROOT/logs` are therefore empty, and its Slurm log holds a few lines: that is expected.
- **Every job times GPFS against the node's disk** once, before it measures (`probe.tsv`), so the reply shows what the step overhead is today on every node the request used. A GPFS that takes more than thirty seconds for one of those timings is not probed again in that job, so the probe cannot hold a job either.

## 2. Two jobs per cluster, submitted together

On `grace1.bsc.es`, then on `glogin1.bsc.es` with the second line's values:

```sh

C=grace; P=ngpp; Q=ngp_bsccs; QA=ngp_debug; N=144; M=--mem=0     # on glogin1.bsc.es: C=mn5; P=gpp; Q=gp_bsccs; QA=gp_debug; N=112; M=
mkdir -p $ROOT/logs/$C
for rep in a b; do
  qos=$Q; [ $rep = a ] && qos=$QA
  sbatch --account=bsc18 --partition=$P --qos=$qos --nodes=1 --ntasks=1 --exclusive --cpus-per-task=$N --hint=nomultithread $M \
    --time=02:00:00 --job-name=v11-$rep --output=$ROOT/logs/$C/run-$rep-%j.out \
    $ROOT/kit/job.sh run $C $ROOT $rep
done
squeue -u $USER -h -o '%i %j %T %M %r %N'
```

The two jobs are the same measurement in two orders, `a` and `b`, on whatever two nodes the scheduler gives them. Neither waits for the other.

**Job `a` asks for the debug QOS**, because it is the one that tells whether the machinery works here, and the debug queue is short. Nicolas does not know that QOS's limits. `sacctmgr show qos $QA format=name,maxwall,maxtrespu,maxjobspu,maxsubmitpu -P` shows them. If it does not take a two-hour job with a node to itself:
- if only its time limit is shorter, submit `a` there with that limit and add `--export=ALL,KIT_BUDGET_SECS=<the limit in seconds, less 600>`: the job then ends `RUN INCOMPLETE` and continues when it is submitted again (step 3);
- otherwise submit `a` with `$Q`, as `b`.

**Whichever starts first, start the rest as soon as you can.** If `a` has ended `RUN DONE` and `b` is still waiting in `$Q` with no start in sight, cancel `b` and submit it in the debug QOS too. The reverse holds as well.

What one job does, on its node's disk:
1. **The runner.** If no job has built it for this cluster yet, it unpacks the bundle, builds the runner offline with the `gcc` and `rust` modules (`cargo build --offline --locked --release`, the flags of the earlier requests), runs v11's unit tests, stores the binary under `$ROOT/builds/$C/` and runs the **smoke test**: one short trial of a few cells of every plan. Otherwise it copies the binary that is there. A job that starts while the other is still building waits for it, thirty minutes at most, and uses its binary, so that both jobs measure with one binary. If both start within the same few seconds, both build; that is expected, and every trial records the hash of the binary it ran (the tables name it).
2. **The five plans, in order**, each to its own result file on the node, stored as `$ROOT/results/$C/<plan>-<rep>.jsonl` when the job ends:

| plan | the question | what it runs | trials per job |
|---|---|---|---|
| `correct` | does v11 deliver every element exactly once here? | v11 at five block lengths, single elements and batches of 16, 256 and 4,096, at nine shapes from `1p1c` to the whole node, with 8-byte and 128-byte elements; two cells with twice as many threads as cores; three other queues as a control | 384 |
| `against` | where does v11 stand beside the other strict FIFOs? | throughput at nine shapes, six on one socket with memory bound to it and three on both with memory interleaved: v11, RBBQ, SegQueue, LSCQ, LBBQ and a mutex over a deque with single elements; v11, LBBQ and the mutex with batches of 256; with no delay and with 10 and 100 ns of work on each side of every element | 693 |
| `inside` | what moves v11 itself? | v11 alone at five shapes: seven batch sizes from 1 to 4,096; four block lengths from 1,024 to 65,536 slots; and, at all nine shapes, the arm that keeps its counters | 603 |
| `memory` | what does each queue hold? | filled to 2^20 elements, drained, and drained and flushed: v11, RBBQ, SegQueue, LBBQ and the mutex | 72 |
| `arrive` | what share of what arrives does each queue deliver? | an element arrives at each producer at a fixed rate, whatever the producer is doing, from one every 10 µs down to one every 3 ns; a producer pushes all that has arrived, as one batch (v11 at its default block of 16,384 slots and with blocks of 65,536, LBBQ, the mutex) or one element at a time (v11, RBBQ, LSCQ, SegQueue, the mutex); consumers take what is there; at the nine shapes; each trial also gives the time from an element's arrival to its delivery | 1,152 |

A shape such as `16p16c` is sixteen producers and sixteen consumers; the shapes are computed on the node from its cores. Two kinds of cell are left out of `against` because a trial of them lasts minutes: LSCQ and the mutex with one producer and 32 or more consumers on single elements. One kind is left out of `arrive`: with one consumer and several producers, the intervals at which more than 500 million elements a second arrive in all, where a queue only grows to its bound.

In `arrive` a queue that cannot keep up is the expected result of many cells, not a failure: its trial ends when its window does and records the share it delivered.

## What Nicolas expects, written before the runs

These are predictions from two machines at home (a 160-core Neoverse-N1 and an eight-core x86), built with this request's flags, to be scored when the data return. They are not instructions. Predictions 2 to 8 were measured with the first issue's source; the one change to v11 since (a batched pop returns what its first claim took, where it went on claiming until its buffer was full) moved no rate of those plans by more than 6 % either way at home.

1. **`correct`.** v11 delivers exactly once in every cell on both clusters, at every block length, the oversubscribed cells included; those may be slow.
2. **`against`, Grace, single elements, no delay.** v11 is far ahead of SegQueue from sixteen threads a side upwards. Beside the pre-sized RBBQ it is level or just ahead at the balanced shapes (1.0 to 1.1 times on the Arm machine at home) and ahead with many producers and one consumer (1.25 times on one socket at home, 1.7 on both). It is behind LSCQ at sixteen threads a side and at half a socket a side (about 0.8 of it at home), and behind LBBQ with one producer and many consumers (0.8 of it).
3. **`against`, MareNostrum 5, single elements.** SegQueue is ahead of v11 at the small shapes, four times at `4p4c` with no delay on the x86 machine at home. Whether that holds at 28 and 56 threads a side is what the request is for.
4. **`against`, with 100 ns of work on each side.** With batches of 256, v11 and LBBQ are within about a tenth of the limit at every one-socket shape. With single elements v11 reaches about 0.8 of the limit at `1p1c` and with many producers and one consumer, and a small part of it at the balanced shapes, where the limit grows with the number of producers and the queues separate.
5. **`inside`, by batch.** With no delay v11's rate grows with the batch at every shape: at sixteen threads a side, 11 M elements a second with single elements, 150 with batches of 16, 1,900 with 256 and 6,500 with 4,096 at home. With 10 ns a side its share of the limit passes 0.7 at batches of 256 at sixteen a side, and only at 1,024 at half a socket a side. With 100 ns a side it passes 0.9 at batches of 64 on one socket (at 1,024 at half a socket a side), and on both sockets it reaches 0.8 only at 4,096.
6. **`inside`, by block length.** With single elements the block's length changes nothing, within a few percent from 1,024 to 65,536 slots. With batches of 256 and no delay, longer blocks are faster: 3.4 to 7 times from 1,024 to 16,384 slots on the Arm machine at home, and faster again at 65,536 at half a socket a side (1.25 to 1.5 times) and on both sockets (1.4 times). On both sockets v11 at its default length is behind LBBQ with batches of 256 (0.8 of it at home) and at 65,536 slots ahead of it (1.15 times); at half a socket a side the two were level within their spread. On eight x86 cores the whole range was 1.2 to 1.35 times.
7. **`inside`, the counters.** With one producer and many consumers on single elements, v11's consumers fail three to four swaps of their head for every element they take (3.8 at `1p79c` at home, 2.8 at `1p7c` on the x86 machine); at the balanced shapes about one; with batches of 256, under 0.2. At a block's end the producers' head stays exhausted for about 0.3 µs with one producer, about 3 µs with a socket's producers, and 33 to 38 µs when the producers are on both sockets.
8. **`memory`.** Drained and flushed keeping no block, v11 holds two blocks (about 0.3 MiB). The pre-sized RBBQ gives nothing back when it is drained: its resident memory stays where the fill left it and grows towards its 16 MiB of slots as the ring is used.
9. **Grace's address space.** The short-block arm of `correct` may fail on Grace with "no address left" if its kernel gives a process fewer than 48 bits of address; the job's `node.txt` records the highest address it saw.
10. **Where the time goes.** The slowest cells are SegQueue's on both sockets with single elements (up to 28 s a trial on the Arm machine at home). A job took 91 minutes at home when it built the runner and 75 when it did not; `arrive` is 28 minutes of either.
11. **`arrive`, what the batch buys.** With a consumer for each producer, v11 with batches delivers every arrival down to an element every 3 ns at each producer at the shapes up to sixteen a side (5.3 thousand million elements a second at sixteen a side on the Arm machine at home). No arm that pushes one element at a time delivers them all below an element every 3 µs at sixteen a side, or every microsecond at four a side. That is two to three orders of magnitude, and it is what this plan is for.
12. **`arrive`, the arms with batches beside each other, on one socket.** LBBQ keeps up as far as v11 or one step short of it up to sixteen a side (at home v11 to 3 ns and LBBQ to 10 at `16p16c`). At half a socket a side LBBQ went further than v11 at its default block at home: every arrival at 10 ns and about 60 % at 3 ns, where v11 delivered 86 to 99 % and 29 %; v11 with blocks of 65,536 slots was level with LBBQ there (100 and 57 %). The mutex with batches stops one to two orders of magnitude before them, and below that delivers almost nothing while its producers go on pushing.
13. **`arrive`, the arms that push one element at a time.** At the balanced shapes on the Arm machine LSCQ delivers the largest share, then v11, then RBBQ, then SegQueue and the mutex (at `16p16c` and an element every microsecond: 86, 70, 29, 13 and 13 %). On x86 SegQueue leads them at the small shapes (99 % at `4p4c` and 100 ns, v11 23 %), and it may deliver less at a lighter load than at a heavier one (86 % at 1 µs and 99 % at 100 ns at home). With many producers and one consumer v11 and RBBQ lead (45 and 39 % at `64p1c` and 1 µs at home, LSCQ 4.5, SegQueue 0.9). With one producer and many consumers v11 leads.
14. **`arrive`, the wait and the batch.** Where v11 with batches keeps up at sixteen a side, half of its elements are delivered within 4 to 9 µs of arriving and 99 in 100 within 8 to 24 µs (the Arm machine at home, from an element every 3 µs down to every 10 ns); at four a side on x86, half within about a microsecond. LBBQ's wait about as long. The batch sets itself: at sixteen a side at home a push carried 1, 3, 9, 27, 87, 292 and 1,229 elements at an element every 3 µs, 1 µs, 300, 100, 30, 10 and 3 ns.
15. **`arrive`, both sockets, and the block's length.** With a socket's producers and a socket's consumers on both sockets, every arm with batches stops early at home (`80p80c`, 2 to 6 trials a cell). v11 at its default block delivers every arrival down to an element every 300 ns and then 48, 14, 4.8 and 1.4 % at 100, 30, 10 and 3 ns; LBBQ 66, 24, 7.9 and 2.4 %; v11 with blocks of 65,536 slots every arrival at 100 ns and then 47, 16 and 4.6 %, twice LBBQ's share. Where v11 falls behind there, each pop carries one whole block and no more. Where the three keep up there (an element every 10 µs down to every microsecond), half of their elements wait 105 to 190 µs, ten to twenty times the wait at half a socket a side. No arm that pushes one element at a time delivers more than a fifth of an element every 10 µs there. With one producer and the whole node's consumers long blocks also go further (99 % at 10 ns and 37 at 3 ns, against 66 and 19 for the default block and 36 and 11 for LBBQ). With the whole node's producers and one consumer they go less far (85 and 77 % at an element every 3 µs and every microsecond, against 93 and 88 for the default block and 91 and 81 for LBBQ). Up to sixteen a side the block's length changes nothing in this plan.
